<p>Switzerland's internet domain is <code>.ch</code>, and its currency is written as <code>CHF</code>, but neither starts with the English word "Switzerland".</p>

      <p>The <code>CH</code> comes from <em>Confoederatio Helvetica</em>, the Latin name for the Swiss Confederation. Latin is useful here because Switzerland has German, French, Italian, and Romansh as national languages, so it avoids choosing one language over the others.</p>

      <p><em>Helvetica</em> here means something like "Helvetian" or "Swiss". The word points back to <em>Helvetia</em>, a Latin name associated with the land, and ultimately to the Helvetii, a Celtic tribe that lived on the Swiss Plateau before and during the Roman period. So there is a small ancient-history layer hiding inside a modern country code.</p>

      <p><code>CHF</code> follows the same logic. In the ISO currency code, <code>CH</code> identifies Switzerland and <code>F</code> stands for franc. So CHF is basically "Switzerland + franc" compressed into three letters.</p>

      <div class="callout">
        <strong>Small distinction:</strong> people often remember CHF as <em>Confoederatio Helvetica Franc</em>. That is a useful shortcut, but the technical ISO explanation is even simpler: country code <code>CH</code> plus <code>F</code> for franc.
      </div>

      <p>It is one of those tiny naming choices where politics, language, and standards quietly meet each other.</p>
