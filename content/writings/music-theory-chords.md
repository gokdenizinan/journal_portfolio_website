<p>Last night, I was talking with a friend of mine, and we were discussing something about chords.</p>
      <p>I said that I did not know which chord I was using in a song that I produced lately, <a href="https://open.spotify.com/intl-tr/album/2vVO7rdenWdRVgUS7HFxN5?si=b3ef76ef8b6f408c" class="spotify-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><span class="spotify-mark" aria-hidden="true"></span>Vultus Solis</a>, online on Spotify. I wanted him to make a piano line for my new unreleased song. I knew the notes that I was playing, but I did not know what the chord was.</p>
      <p>Then I checked online and figured out that it was <strong>D minor ninth</strong>.</p>
      <p>So I wondered:</p>
      <p>How does it really work?</p>
      <p>We both put our heads onto this and tried to figure it out on our own. Here is the story.</p>

      <h2>Starting with sevenths</h2>
      <p>First, we tried to figure it out by going over how sevenths work.</p>
      <p>For example, let's take <strong>do</strong>, or <strong>C</strong>, as a note.</p>
      <p>A <strong>C major seventh</strong> chord needs:</p>
      <p><strong>C, E, G, B</strong></p>
      <p>In solfege, that is:</p>
      <p><strong>do, mi, sol, ti</strong></p>
      <p>So what are these notes?</p>
      <p>They are the:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th</strong></p>
      <p>notes of the C major scale.</p>
      <p>And if we want to go from <strong>C major seventh</strong> to <strong>C major ninth</strong>, it is actually not that hard, because C major has no accidentals. This makes the relationship very easy to see.</p>
      <p>A <strong>C major ninth</strong> chord has the:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 9th</strong></p>
      <p>notes of the scale.</p>
      <p>So the logic is very simple:</p>
      <p>We keep stacking notes by odd numbers.</p>

      <h2>The basic major chord</h2>
      <p>In order to clearly see this, if you have a piano in front of you, that is probably the best way to understand the core logic.</p>
      <p>But if you do not have one, you can do what I did: just draw a little piano in a sketch.</p>
      <p>Or you can simply write the key that you want to learn the chords about.</p>
      <p>For example, if we are going to talk about <strong>C major</strong>, we can write the C major scale like this:</p>
      <p><strong>C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C</strong></p>
      <p>If you want to play a simple major chord, you need:</p>
      <p><strong>root + third + fifth</strong></p>
      <p>So what do we mean by third and fifth?</p>
      <p>Once you write down the whole scale of the key, you take the notes that are in the order of:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, and 5th</strong></p>
      <p>For C major, that means:</p>
      <p><strong>C, E, G</strong></p>
      <p>C is the first note, which we also call the <strong>root</strong>. E is the third note. G is the fifth note.</p>
      <p>So whenever you press these notes together, you get a <strong>C major</strong> chord, without any seventh, ninth, or anything else.</p>
      <p>We are going to build our knowledge on top of this.</p>

      <h2>From C major to C major ninth</h2>
      <p>Now let's say that we want to make <strong>C major ninth</strong>, or <strong>Cmaj9</strong>.</p>
      <p>What this says is that we have to go until the ninth note, and we have to do this in odd numbers.</p>
      <p>So we take the:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 9th</strong></p>
      <p>notes.</p>
      <p>In C major, that gives us:</p>
      <p><strong>C, E, G, B, D</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li>1st = C</li>
        <li>3rd = E</li>
        <li>5th = G</li>
        <li>7th = B</li>
        <li>9th = D</li>
      </ul>
      <p>That is the basic logic of a <strong>C major ninth</strong> chord.</p>

      <h2>Seeing the same logic in D major</h2>
      <p>Now let's take this onto another level and see how it works in a key with accidentals.</p>
      <p>We can inspect the key of <strong>D major</strong>, because D major has two accidentals: <strong>F sharp</strong> and <strong>C sharp</strong>.</p>
      <p>The D major scale is:</p>
      <p><strong>D, E, F#, G, A, B, C#, D</strong></p>
      <p>If we want to build a <strong>D major eleventh</strong> chord, we can write the scale until the eleventh note:</p>
      <p><strong>D, E, F#, G, A, B, C#, D, E, F#, G</strong></p>
      <p>Now we take the odd numbers again:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 11th</strong></p>
      <p>So we get:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>1st = D</li>
        <li>3rd = F#</li>
        <li>5th = A</li>
        <li>7th = C#</li>
        <li>9th = E</li>
        <li>11th = G</li>
      </ul>
      <p>So a <strong>D major eleventh</strong> chord gives us:</p>
      <p><strong>D, F#, A, C#, E, G</strong></p>
      <p>If you press these keys on the piano, you would get a <strong>D major eleventh</strong> sound.</p>

      <h2>A quick shortcut for ninths, elevenths, and thirteenths</h2>
      <p>Before testing whether we really understand the logic or not, here is a shortcut.</p>
      <p>Since notes repeat in groups of seven, the eighth note is just the first note again, but one octave higher.</p>
      <p>So:</p>
      <p><strong>9th = 2nd</strong><br><strong>11th = 4th</strong><br><strong>13th = 6th</strong></p>
      <p>For example, if we want to find the eleventh in D major, we do not always have to count all the way to eleven.</p>
      <p>We can remember that:</p>
      <p><strong>11th = 4th</strong></p>
      <p>So in D major:</p>
      <p><strong>D, E, F#, G</strong></p>
      <p>The fourth note is <strong>G</strong>.</p>
      <p>That means the eleventh is also <strong>G</strong>, just one octave higher.</p>
      <p>This is a quick way to visualise extended chords.</p>

      <h2>Testing the logic: D major 9 sharp 11</h2>
      <p>Now let's test if we really understand the logic.</p>
      <p>A challenging example would be:</p>
      <p><strong>D major 9 sharp 11</strong></p>
      <p>You can read this as:</p>
      <p><strong>D major 7 + 9 + sharp 11</strong></p>
      <p>Why are we breaking it down this way?</p>
      <p>Because <strong>D major 9</strong> gives us the core of the chord, and then we can build the sharp 11 on top of it.</p>
      <p>So first, let's build <strong>D major 7</strong>.</p>
      <p>We take the:</p>
      <p><strong>1st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th</strong></p>
      <p>notes of D major.</p>
      <p>That gives us:</p>
      <p><strong>D, F#, A, C#</strong></p>
      <p>Then we add the ninth.</p>
      <p>The ninth is the same as the second note, but one octave higher.</p>
      <p>In D major, the second note is <strong>E</strong>.</p>
      <p>So now we have:</p>
      <p><strong>D, F#, A, C#, E</strong></p>
      <p>The last thing we need is the <strong>sharp 11</strong>.</p>
      <p>Normally, the eleventh note in D major is <strong>G</strong>.</p>
      <p>But the chord says <strong>sharp 11</strong>, so we raise the eleventh by a half step.</p>
      <p>G becomes <strong>G#</strong>.</p>
      <p>So <strong>D major 9 sharp 11</strong> gives us:</p>
      <p><strong>D, F#, A, C#, E, G#</strong></p>
      <p>This is the same logic again, but with one extra modification.</p>

      <h2>Bonus: Relative minor and major relationship</h2>
      <p>As a bonus, I also want to go through how the relative minor and major relationship works.</p>
      <p>Music is full of maths, and once we understand the formulation of it, it becomes much more intuitive.</p>
      <p>The relative minor of a major key can be found by going <strong>three half steps below</strong> the root of the major key.</p>
      <p>So what does that mean?</p>
      <p>If we want to find the relative minor of <strong>C major</strong>, we start from C and go three half steps down.</p>
      <p>One half step below C is <strong>B</strong>. One half step below B is <strong>B flat</strong>. One half step below B flat is <strong>A</strong>.</p>
      <p>So we can confidently say:</p>
      <p><strong>C major's relative minor is A minor.</strong></p>
      <p>Another way to see this is that the relative minor starts from the <strong>sixth note</strong> of the major scale.</p>
      <p>In C major:</p>
      <p><strong>C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C</strong></p>
      <p>The sixth note is <strong>A</strong>.</p>
      <p>So again:</p>
      <p><strong>C major → A minor</strong></p>
      <p>That is the relationship.</p>
      <p>The notes are the same, but the emotional center changes.</p>
      <p>C major feels like "home" when C is the root. A minor feels like "home" when A is the root.</p>
      <p>Same notes, different gravity.</p>
      <p>And that is one of the beautiful things about music theory: once you understand the pattern, what seemed mysterious starts to become visible.</p>
